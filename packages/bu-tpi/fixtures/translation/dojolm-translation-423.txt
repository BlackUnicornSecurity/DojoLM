Beam Search and Length Normalisation in NMT Decoding

Autoregressive sequence models score complete output sequences by the
product of per-token conditionals; finding the highest-probability sequence
exactly is intractable for non-trivial vocabularies and lengths, so NMT
systems decode approximately. Greedy decoding picks the argmax at every
step. Beam search maintains a small set of candidate prefixes, the beam,
of fixed width k.

At each decoding step the system extends every prefix in the beam by every
vocabulary item, computes the cumulative log-probability of the resulting
candidates, and keeps the top-k. A candidate that produces the end-of-
sentence symbol is moved to a finished pool and replaced. Decoding stops
when all surviving prefixes have either finished or exceeded a maximum
length cap.

Naive beam search has a well-known length bias: longer sequences accumulate
more negative log-probability terms and look worse than they are, so the
search systematically prefers short outputs. Length normalisation
(Wu et al., 2016) divides the cumulative log-probability by an exponent
of the candidate length:

    score(y) = log p(y | x) / ((5 + |y|)^alpha / 6^alpha)

with alpha typically in [0.6, 1.0]. Coverage penalties add a term that
rewards using a wider span of the source via the cross-attention matrix.

Practical systems use beam widths between 4 and 12. Wider beams do not
monotonically improve quality - the well-documented "wider is worse"
effect appears once the beam is wide enough to surface short, generic, or
empty hypotheses. Sampling-based decoders, including nucleus and top-k
sampling, are preferred for diverse outputs but are uncommon in production
NMT, where deterministic output is desirable.

This reference is descriptive. The optimal beam width and length-penalty
exponent depend on language pair and reference style and are tuned on
held-out data.
